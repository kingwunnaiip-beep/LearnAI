Big picture

How ecosystems work

This chapter links organisms to their habitats, populations and communities, then follows energy through food chains and nutrients through carbon and nitrogen cycles. It also examines population growth and limiting factors.

  • Energy enters ecosystems mainly from sunlight and flows through trophic levels.
  • Nutrients are recycled between organisms and the environment.
  • Population size changes when conditions alter birth, death, immigration and emigration.
Remember the contrast: energy flows through ecosystems, but nutrients cycle.

Area 2 Energy and recycling

  • The Sun is the principal energy input for most ecosystems.
  • Producers convert light energy into chemical energy by photosynthesis.
  • Consumers obtain energy by feeding.
  • Decomposers break down dead and waste material.
  • Energy is lost between trophic levels, so food chains are limited in length.
  • Carbon and nitrogen compounds are recycled.

Energy transfer and ecological pyramids

The Chapter 18 source covers energy losses, the approximate 10% rule, food-chain length and pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy.

Why energy decreases

  • Respiration releases energy as heat.
  • Energy is used in movement and muscle activity.
  • Energy leaves in excretory products.
  • Some food is not eaten.
  • Some material is egested in faeces.
only a fraction of energy becomes biomass available to the next trophic level

Carbon and nitrogen cycles

Nutrients are repeatedly recycled between organisms and the environment.

Carbon cycle

Atmospheric CO₂Carbon dioxide is available to producers.
PhotosynthesisRemoves carbon dioxide and builds organic compounds.
FeedingTransfers carbon compounds through food chains.
RespirationReturns carbon dioxide to the atmosphere.
DecompositionDecomposers respire while breaking down dead material.
CombustionBurning fossil fuels / biomass releases carbon dioxide.
Photosynthesis is the major carbon-dioxide removal step; respiration, decomposition and combustion return CO₂.

Carbon-cycle visual

Nitrogen cycle

  1. Nitrogen fixation: nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen into useful nitrogen compounds; the source also lists lightning.
  2. Plant uptake: roots absorb nitrate ions for making amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids.
  3. Feeding: nitrogen-containing compounds move through food chains.
  4. Ammonification: decomposers break down proteins and nitrogenous wastes to ammonium compounds.
  5. Nitrification: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium compounds to nitrates in aerobic conditions.
  6. Denitrification: denitrifying bacteria convert nitrates to nitrogen gas, especially in waterlogged / low-oxygen conditions.

Population growth and limiting factors

The Chapter 18 source includes sigmoid growth, lag / log / stationary / death phases, carrying capacity, immigration, emigration and limiting factors. June 2026 Paper 43 directly tested phytoplankton growth phases.

Population-growth curve

LagSlow growth while organisms adjust and begin reproducing.
Log / exponentialRapid reproduction when resources are abundant.
StationaryPopulation fluctuates around carrying capacity as limiting factors act.
Death / declineDeaths exceed births when conditions deteriorate.

What changes population size?

population change = births + immigration − deaths − emigration
  • Abiotic limits: temperature, water and light.
  • Biotic limits: food, predation, disease and competition.
  • Carrying capacity: the maximum population size the environment can sustain under given conditions.
At carrying capacity, population size may fluctuate rather than staying perfectly constant.

Predator–prey oscillations

The source highlights the classic snowshoe hare–lynx example and the idea that predator numbers tend to follow prey numbers with a time lag.

  • More prey can support more predators.
  • Predator increase may later reduce prey numbers.
  • Reduced prey can then cause predator numbers to fall.
  • Cycles may repeat.

2026 exam spotlight

Paper 43 measured phytoplankton population as organisms per cm³, asked for a population calculation in 0.5 dm³, then asked candidates to identify and explain the phases of the growth curve.

For graph questions: read the trend first, then link each phase to reproduction, resources, competition and limiting factors.

Exam trap detector

Arrow direction
Trap

Food-chain arrows point from food to feeder — the direction of energy transfer.

Energy ≠ nutrients
Trap

Energy is not recycled; nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen are recycled.

Population ≠ community
Trap

A population contains one species; a community contains populations of different species.

Stationary ≠ no activity
Trap

At the stationary phase, organisms still reproduce and die; population size is limited around carrying capacity.

Exam-style questions with model answers

These use the terminology and topic structure found in the Chapter 18 source and recent 0610 exam material.

Q1. Define a population.

A group of organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time.

Q2. Define a community.

Populations of different species living together in the same area.

Q3. Define an ecosystem.

A community of organisms interacting with each other and with the physical environment.

Q4. What is a producer?

An organism that makes its own organic nutrients using energy, usually light energy in photosynthesis.

Q5. What is a decomposer?

An organism, such as a bacterium or fungus, that obtains energy by breaking down dead or waste organic matter.

Q6. What does an arrow mean in a food chain?

It shows the direction of energy transfer from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.

Q7. Define trophic level.

The feeding position of an organism in a food chain, food web or ecological pyramid.

Q8. Give three reasons energy is lost between trophic levels.

Any three of: heat released by respiration, movement, excretion, egestion, uneaten parts.

Q9. Why are food chains usually short?

Energy is lost at each trophic transfer, so too little remains to support many higher trophic levels.

Q10. Distinguish pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy.

Numbers show organism counts; biomass shows total dry mass at each trophic level; energy pyramids show energy flow through trophic levels.

Q11. State three processes that return carbon dioxide to the atmosphere.

Respiration, decomposition and combustion.

Q12. What do nitrifying bacteria do?

They convert ammonium compounds into nitrates in aerobic conditions.

Q13. What do denitrifying bacteria do?

They convert nitrates to nitrogen gas, especially in low-oxygen / waterlogged conditions.

Q14. What is carrying capacity?

The maximum population size that an environment can sustain under the existing conditions.

Q15. Name two abiotic and two biotic limiting factors.

Abiotic: any two of temperature, water, light. Biotic: any two of food, predation, disease, competition.

Q16. Describe the log phase of a population-growth curve.

Population size increases rapidly because reproduction is fast while resources and conditions are favourable and limiting factors are relatively weak.
